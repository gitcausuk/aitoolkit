Accessible text alternative · Version 2.3

Status and scope
This is practical professional guidance developed at Saïd Business School. It supports,
and does not replace, current University policy, Examination Regulations, local approval
processes, accessibility requirements or programme guidance.
Use this playbook to move from the intended learning to clear AI permissions, visible
evidence of student learning and copy-ready assessment wording. It covers common
assessment types rather than prescribing one University-wide design.
Assessment-design principle
Design assessments that produce verifiable judgement.
The central question is not simply "Did AI write this?" but whether the assessment provides credible
evidence that the student can reach a reasoned conclusion and explain, defend and apply it. VIVA is
the practical framework for designing that evidence.
The five-step workflow
1 Clarify the purpose
State the knowledge, reasoning, judgement, performance or professional capability the assessment
should evidence.
2 Choose the permission level
Decide whether AI is not permitted, limited, open or integral. These are toolkit categories for
communication, not mandatory University-wide labels.
3 Apply VIVA
Check that the design is valid, inclusive, visible and accountable, underpinned by integrity, honesty and
transparency.
4 Write the rule and declaration
Issue clear written guidance in advance for the discrete assessment. Specify permitted, required and
prohibited uses and the declaration expected.
5 Mark, review and improve
Align criteria with the intended learning, review the assessment after use and update the design when
evidence, student experience or technology changes.

Permission levels are a shared language, not an assessment design in themselves.
Their value depends on alignment with the intended learning, disciplinary context,
equitable access, staff capacity, marking and workable implementation.
Implementation safeguards
Process evidence is evidence, not proof of authorship. Oral follow-up is not a universal gold standard.
Where AI could materially obscure the capability being assessed, draw on more than one
proportionate source of evidence, such as the submitted work together with staged drafts, explanation
or dialogue. Triangulation strengthens the judgement; no single component proves authorship.
Any oral or additional component that affects marks should be specified in advance, consistently
applied, accessible, proportionate and locally approved. Do not selectively viva only students
suspected of AI use.
Practical professional guidance, not University policy 2 / 5
Assessment types
Timed exams and in-class tests
1. Define whether the task tests recall, method, reasoning,
interpretation or application.
2. Use approved controlled conditions and explain why AI
is not permitted, if that is the decision.
3. Require workings, justification or application rather than
final answers alone.
4. Check accessibility, reasonable adjustments, time
pressure and platform reliability.
5. Rotate or refresh questions for assessment quality, not
because novelty makes them inaccessible to AI.
Copy-ready wording — adapt the brackets
AI use is not permitted in this assessment
because independent performance under
controlled conditions is part of the capability
being assessed. [State any authorised assistive
technology or reasonable-adjustment
arrangements.]
Take-home essays
1. State the argument, synthesis or disciplinary judgement
the essay should evidence.
2. Choose the AI permission level before deciding whether
drafts, plans or reflections are needed.
3. Use specific course material, comparison, application or
a justified critical position.
4. Ask for proportionate process evidence only when it
contributes to learning or assessment validity.
5. Align criteria with reasoning, evidence and judgement
rather than polish alone.
Copy-ready wording — adapt the brackets
AI may be used only for [permitted purposes]. It
must not be used to [prohibited purposes]. Your
final argument, interpretation and use of
evidence must be your own. Include the
prescribed declaration before the references.
Literature reviews
1. Decide whether literature discovery, screening, synthesis
or writing is being assessed.
2. Require a transparent search strategy appropriate to
the discipline.
3. Ask students to verify every AI-suggested source against
the original record.
4. Use annotated source notes or inclusion/exclusion
reasoning where these evidence the learning outcome.
5. Mark synthesis, evaluation and judgement, not the
number of sources found.
Copy-ready wording — adapt the brackets
Where AI is used for literature discovery or
organisation, verify each source against the
original publication and declare the tool, purpose
and verification process. AI-generated citations
must not be treated as verified sources.
Case studies and policy memos
1. Clarify whether the task tests diagnosis,
recommendation, trade-offs, evidence use or
communication to an audience.
2. Use supplied data and a specific decision context rather
than relying on novelty or secrecy.
3. Require a prioritised recommendation and
explicit reasoning.
4. Make assumptions, limitations and trade-offs visible.
5. Use an oral or written defence only where proportionate
and consistently designed.
Copy-ready wording — adapt the brackets
Your recommendation must be justified using the
supplied evidence and explicit trade-offs. AI may
be used for [purposes], but you remain
responsible for the analysis, source verification
and final recommendation.
Practical professional guidance, not University policy 3 / 5
Assessment types continued
Presentations and posters
1. Decide whether the assessment tests content,
communication, visual design, oral response or all four.
2. State whether AI may support research, scripting, slide
design or rehearsal.
3. Use individual questioning or explanation where it is part
of the intended learning.
4. Provide consistent criteria and reasonable adjustments
for any live component.
5. For groups, include a contribution statement and an
individual evidence component where appropriate.
Copy-ready wording — adapt the brackets
AI may be used for [preparation tasks]. It may
not be used during the live assessed component
unless explicitly stated. Be prepared to explain
the evidence, choices and recommendations in
your presentation.
Group projects
1. Separate the team product from the individual learning
that needs to be evidenced.
2. Use milestone decisions, role records or contribution
statements proportionately.
3. Specify whether the AI permission applies to the team,
each member or both.
4. Avoid process logging that creates excessive
surveillance or workload.
5. Use peer or individual evidence with transparent criteria.
Copy-ready wording — adapt the brackets
Each team member must submit a short
contribution and AI-use statement identifying
their role, any substantive AI assistance and how
outputs were checked. The team remains jointly
responsible for the final submission.
Problem sets and quantitative work
1. Identify whether the learning lies in method selection,
calculation, interpretation or modelling judgement.
2. Require workings and conceptual explanation where
they provide relevant evidence.
3. Decide whether AI may check, generate or explain code
and calculations.
4. Use parameter variation for breadth and feedback, not
as a guarantee against AI use.
5. Mark method, assumptions, error checking
and interpretation.
Copy-ready wording — adapt the brackets
AI may be used for
[checking/formatting/debugging] but not to
generate the submitted solution. Show your
workings and explain why the selected method is
appropriate. Declare any substantive AI
assistance.
Coding and data analysis
1. Decide whether syntax, architecture, debugging,
analysis, interpretation or professional use of AI is
being assessed.
2. Require explanation of key design and
analytical choices.
3. Use version history, notebooks or demonstrations where
they support the learning outcome.
4. Require comments or a declaration for substantive AI-
generated code.
5. Mark understanding, testing, reproducibility, limitations
and interpretation.
Copy-ready wording — adapt the brackets
AI coding tools may be used for [permitted tasks].
You must understand, test and be able to explain
all submitted code. Identify substantive AI-
generated or AI-modified code and describe how
it was verified.
Practical professional guidance, not University policy 4 / 5
Research proposals and
dissertations
1. Define permitted AI use by stage: question development,
literature work, methods, analysis, drafting and editing.
2. Use supervision and milestones as developmental
evidence, not forensic proof.
3. Apply both assessment and research requirements
where relevant.
4. Address data protection, confidentiality, ethics, IP, funder
and publisher rules.
5. Require a declaration in the location and form
prescribed locally.
Copy-ready wording — adapt the brackets
AI use is governed by the written assessment
guidance, the University research policy where
applicable and local departmental requirements.
Declare substantive use, verify outputs and
remain responsible for the research design,
evidence, analysis and final text.
AI-integral critique or simulation
1. Make the AI-related capability an explicit
learning outcome.
2. Provide equal baseline access to the required tool or a
suitable alternative.
3. Assess prompting only where it is pedagogically
relevant; prioritise evaluation and judgement.
4. Require verification against disciplinary sources and
identification of limitations.
5. Plan for tool failure, model variation and accessibility.
Copy-ready wording — adapt the brackets
Use of [tool/capability] is required for this
assessment. Your marks will be based on how
well you evaluate, verify, improve and take
responsibility for the output, not on the fluency
of the AI-generated content alone.
Minimum quality-assurance check
Seven statements that should be true before the assessment is issued.
The intended learning and the reason for the AI permission level are explicit.
Students receive written guidance before the assessment and know what to declare.
Permitted tools are equitably available or an appropriate alternative is provided.
The design is accessible, proportionate and manageable for students, markers and administrators.
Marking criteria reward the intended knowledge, reasoning, judgement or performance.
No claim is made that a format, process log or oral component proves authorship or is AI-proof.
The design has the necessary local approval and fits Examination Regulations and programme requirements.
